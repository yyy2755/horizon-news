## [Stanford study finds brain arises from two separate progenitor lineages](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2026/09/two-separate-brains.html) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Stanford Medicine-led research identified two distinct progenitor cell lineages that independently form the brain: an Otx2-expressing lineage destined for the forebrain and midbrain, and a Gbx2-expressing lineage committed to the hindbrain. The work, posted as a bioRxiv preprint in July 2025 and published in Nature Neuroscience, also introduced a new method for growing brain stem cells in vitro. The finding challenges the long-held view that the brain descends from a single ancestral progenitor population, suggesting the forebrain/midbrain and hindbrain have separate evolutionary and developmental origins. The accompanying in vitro stem cell culture technique could substantially accelerate research into neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS by making it easier to grow and study human brain stem cells in the lab. The two progenitor populations never overlap and are mutually exclusive from the earliest stages of development, with the anterior neural ectoderm and posterior neural ectoderm emerging simultaneously during gastrulation. The preprint is available under a CC-BY 4.0 license, and the study's key novelty lies in both the separate lineage finding and the new in vitro culture method rather than merely in the fact that brain regions differ.

hackernews · emigre · Sep 19, 05:48 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49763697)

**Background**: During embryonic development, progenitor cells are the early, unspecialized cells that divide and give rise to more mature cell types; in the brain, they were traditionally thought to form a single lineage that later regionalizes into forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain. The forebrain and midbrain handle higher cognition and sensory processing, while the hindbrain controls basic functions like breathing and heart rate. Growing neural stem cells outside the body (in vitro) has historically been difficult, limiting disease modeling; methods like neurospheres have been used but remain imperfect.

## [OpenAI Used Internal LLMs to Design Its Jalapeño Chip](https://spectrum.ieee.org/llms-for-chip-design) ⭐️ 8.0/10

OpenAI fully unveiled Jalapeño on 25 August, its debut AI accelerator chip built with Broadcom, which delivers up to 13.4 petaflops of 4-bit compute and 232 GB of memory bandwidth. After the first chips returned from the foundry in May, OpenAI pointed internal LLMs at writing software for benchmarks like SemiAnalysis's InferenceX, lifting performance on DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention kernel from 0.31% of the theoretical ceiling to 88.94% in roughly 40 hours. This is one of the most concrete public demonstrations that LLMs can meaningfully accelerate chip bring-up and software optimization, not just code completion, which could reshape how hardware teams approach post-silicon validation. It also intensifies the debate over whether AI vendors are using customer engagements to absorb valuable semiconductor IP. OpenAI's Ho confirmed the team used internal LLMs fine-tuned for chip design that are not available to the public, but declined to detail which models were used. The 0.31%-to-88.94% gain is measured against a theoretical ceiling set by the chip's own compute and memory bandwidth, so the benchmark reflects software efficiency rather than raw silicon capability.

**Background**: Jalapeño is OpenAI's first custom AI accelerator, co-developed with Broadcom and aimed specifically at large language model inference rather than training. In chip development, 'bring-up' is the painful post-fabrication phase where engineers get early silicon running and tune software to extract real performance. LLMs are increasingly being applied to electronic design automation (EDA) and related workflows, but using them to optimize benchmark kernels on brand-new hardware is a newer and more striking application.

## [PlanetScale launches Tin, a full-text search extension for Postgres](https://planetscale.com/blog/introducing-tin) ⭐️ 7.0/10

PlanetScale announced Tin (Text INdex), a full-text search extension for Postgres that adds an inverted index type, BM25 ranking, and the TINQL query language. It is currently available only on PlanetScale's cloud Postgres service, with a separate open-source local extension called Lead intended mainly for syntax testing rather than matching cloud performance. The launch reflects a broader trend of database vendors adding search capabilities directly into Postgres, following efforts like ParadeDB's pg_search, Timescale's pg_textsearch, and Neon/Databricks' Lakebase Search. It gives PlanetScale customers a managed alternative to native Postgres full-text search or external engines like Elasticsearch, though it also raises questions about lock-in and whether it beats built-in options. Tin is built around an inverted index and BM25 relevance ranking, and introduces TINQL as its query language; PlanetScale cites vendor benchmarks for its performance. The local open-source version, Lead, is deliberately limited and does not share the same performance characteristics as the cloud offering, meaning users cannot fully evaluate Tin outside PlanetScale's platform.

hackernews · ksec · Sep 19, 13:52 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49766611)

**Background**: Postgres has long shipped built-in full-text search based on tsvector, tsquery, and tsrank, with GIN indexes and query optimization, but many developers find its ranking quality and index sizes limiting compared to dedicated search engines like Elasticsearch or Solr. In recent years, extensions such as ParadeDB's pg_search have added BM25 ranking and richer query languages to Postgres, and cloud providers are now packaging similar capabilities as managed services. BM25 is a standard relevance-ranking algorithm used by search engines, while an inverted index maps terms to the documents containing them for fast lookup.
